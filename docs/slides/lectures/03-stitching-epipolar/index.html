Three tools we already have

Homography (lecture 1)

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u'\\ \tilde v'\\ \tilde w'\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13}\\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23}\\ \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{31}} & \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{32}} & h_{33}\end{bmatrix}}_{H}\begin{bmatrix}u\\ v\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

the projective transformation: lines stay lines, parallelism is lost; defined up to scale → 8 DoF

DLT (lecture 2)

Direct Linear Transform: fit a matrix defined up to scale (e.g. the camera matrix $P$) to correspondences $\tilde{\mathbf u} \equiv P\tilde{\mathbf X}$. Multiplying out the denominators gives equations linear in its entries $\mathbf p$; stacked:

$$A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0:\quad \min \|A\mathbf p\| \ \text{s.t.}\ \|\mathbf p\| = 1$$

→ eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$

RANSAC (lecture 2)

wrong correspondences are normal: fit the model to minimal random samples, keep the one with the most inliers, refit

$$N \ge \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)}$$

The camera as a motion sensor

Points tracked from frame to frame slide across the image: near points sweep long tracks, the far wall hardly moves. From these motions alone the camera's rotation and direction of travel can be computed (Part 3); chained over time they give its trajectory: visual odometry (Part 4). Small errors add up, so the chained path slowly drifts.

Homography: definition

A homography (projective transformation) is an invertible $3\times3$ matrix $H$ acting on homogeneous coordinates:

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u'\\ \tilde v'\\ \tilde w'\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13}\\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23}\\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\ v\\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det H \ne 0$$

Back to pixels: divide by $\tilde w'$:

$$\begin{aligned} u' &= \frac{\tilde u'}{\tilde w'} = \frac{h_{11}u + h_{12}v + h_{13}}{h_{31}u + h_{32}v + h_{33}}\\ v' &= \frac{\tilde v'}{\tilde w'} = \frac{h_{21}u + h_{22}v + h_{23}}{h_{31}u + h_{32}v + h_{33}}\end{aligned}$$

  • $H$ and $kH$ ($k \ne 0$) give the same map: 8 DoF
  • straight lines stay straight; conversely, every invertible map of the (projective) plane that keeps lines straight is a homography (sketch: optional slide)
  • closed under composition ($H_2H_1$) and inverse ($H^{-1}$); $h_{31} = h_{32} = 0$: affine (lecture 1 hierarchy)

Geometric meaning: project one plane onto another through a point $O$ (a central projection). Equal steps on $\Pi_1$ become shrinking steps on $\Pi_2$ (distances and ratios of lengths are not kept), but collinear points stay collinear. Why this is a homography: next (optional) slide.

Central projection is a homography: a sketch optional

1. Plane → plane through $O$ is a homography

Setting: points are vectors in $\mathbb R^3$, the centre $O = \mathbf 0$; $\Pi_1, \Pi_2 \subset \mathbb R^3$ are planes not through $O$.

Coordinates on $\Pi_1$, chosen freely: a point $\mathbf a_1 \in \Pi_1$ (origin) and independent $\mathbf b_1, \mathbf c_1 \in \mathbb R^3$ parallel to $\Pi_1$ (axes). They define the map

$$\varphi_1:\ \mathbb R^2 \to \Pi_1,\quad (s, t) \mapsto \mathbf X = \mathbf a_1 + s\,\mathbf b_1 + t\,\mathbf c_1 = \underbrace{[\,\mathbf b_1\ \mathbf c_1\ \mathbf a_1\,]}_{M_1 \in \mathbb R^{3\times3}}\begin{bmatrix}s\\ t\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

a bijection: every point $\mathbf X \in \Pi_1$ (a vector in $\mathbb R^3$) has exactly one pair $(s, t) \in \mathbb R^2$, its 2D coordinates (on an image plane: the pixel). $M_1$ is invertible: otherwise $\mathbf a_1 \in \operatorname{span}(\mathbf b_1, \mathbf c_1)$ and $O \in \Pi_1$. Likewise $\varphi_2$, $M_2$, $(s', t')$ on $\Pi_2$.

The projection $\pi: \Pi_1 \to \Pi_2$ sends $\mathbf X$ to where the line $\{\lambda\mathbf X : \lambda \in \mathbb R\}$ through $O$ meets $\Pi_2$: $\pi(\mathbf X) = \lambda\mathbf X$ for one number $\lambda$ (depending on $\mathbf X$).

Claim: in coordinates, $(s, t) \mapsto (s', t') = \varphi_2^{-1}\big(\pi(\varphi_1(s, t))\big)$ is a homography:

$$M_2\begin{bmatrix}s'\\ t'\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \lambda M_1\begin{bmatrix}s\\ t\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix}s'\\ t'\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \equiv \underbrace{M_2^{-1}M_1}_{H}\begin{bmatrix}s\\ t\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

$\lambda$ is the scale that homogeneous coordinates ignore. Other $\mathbf a, \mathbf b, \mathbf c$: $H$ times invertible matrices, still a homography. Rays parallel to $\Pi_2$: $\tilde w' = 0$, the horizon.

2. Lines stay lines

Geometry: a line of $\Pi_1$ and $O$ span a plane, which meets $\Pi_2$ in a line. Algebra: if $\mathbf l^\top\tilde{\mathbf u} = 0$, then $(H^{-\top}\mathbf l)^\top (H\tilde{\mathbf u}) = \mathbf l^\top\tilde{\mathbf u} = 0$: the image points lie on the line $H^{-\top}\mathbf l$.

3. Conversely: keeps lines ⇒ homography

  • 4 points, no 3 collinear, and their 4 images determine a unique $H$ (8 equations, 8 DoF: the DLT)
  • let $f$ be a bijection that keeps lines, and $H$ agree with $f$ on 4 such points; then $g = H^{-1}\circ f$ keeps lines and fixes the 4 points
  • lines through fixed points and their intersections stay fixed; repeating this, $g$ acts on each line like an automorphism of the field $\mathbb R$, and the only one is the identity: $g = \mathrm{id}$, so $f = H$

Hartley & Zisserman, sec. 2.3 (projective transformations, Theorem 2.10); 3 is the fundamental theorem of projective geometry.

When are two images related by a homography?

1. Planar scene

any camera motion

plane → image 1 is a homography $H_1$, plane → image 2 is $H_2$, so image 1 → image 2 is $H_2 H_1^{-1}$

2. Pure rotation

any scene, the camera only turns about its centre

the rays do not change, only the image plane does: both red points on a ray land on one pixel (white), in either image

3. Distant scene

baseline ≪ depth

translation hardly matters: almost a pure rotation (aerial and landscape photos)

Otherwise (a 3D scene and a moving camera) there is no homography: points at different depths shift differently (parallax). That case is Part 3.

Why: the formulas

Pure rotation

Camera 1: $\tilde{\mathbf u}_1 \equiv K\mathbf x$. Camera 2 has the same centre, rotated: $\tilde{\mathbf u}_2 \equiv K R\,\mathbf x$.

$$\mathbf x \equiv K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \tilde{\mathbf u}_2 \equiv \underbrace{K R K^{-1}}_{H}\,\tilde{\mathbf u}_1$$

The depth of $\mathbf x$ cancels: one $H$ for every pixel.

Planar scene

World frame on the plane, so $z_w = 0$ (lecture 2, the planar DLT degeneracy):

$$\tilde{\mathbf u} \equiv K\,[\,\mathbf r_1\ \mathbf r_2\ \mathbf r_3\ \mathbf t\,]\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ 0\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{K\,[\,\mathbf r_1\ \mathbf r_2\ \mathbf t\,]}_{3\times3\ \text{homography}}\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

Image 1 → image 2: $H_2 H_1^{-1}$. Zhang's calibration uses exactly these $H_i$.

General form (plane $\mathbf n^\top \mathbf x_1 = d$ in camera 1, camera 2 at $\mathbf x_2 = R\mathbf x_1 + \mathbf t$): $\;\mathbf x_2 = \big(R + \mathbf t\,\mathbf n^\top/d\big)\,\mathbf x_1$, so $H = K\big(R + \mathbf t\,\mathbf n^\top/d\big)K^{-1}$. For $d \to \infty$ (a distant plane) it becomes $KRK^{-1}$.

Distant scene: how far is far enough? optional

Intuition

From a train window the posts by the track rush past, the trees behind them move slowly, and the mountains on the horizon hardly move (the Moon seems to travel with the train). When the camera moves, the image shift shrinks with the depth; when it turns, everything shifts alike.

Formula

A point at depth $Z$ in camera 1: $\mathbf x_1 = Z\,K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_1$; in camera 2: $\mathbf x_2 = R\mathbf x_1 + \mathbf t$. So

$$\tilde{\mathbf u}_2 \equiv K\mathbf x_2 = Z\,KRK^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_1 + K\mathbf t \;\equiv\; \underbrace{KRK^{-1}}_{\text{rotation } H}\,\tilde{\mathbf u}_1 + \frac{1}{Z}\,K\mathbf t$$

The translation enters only through $\mathbf t/Z$. Using $H = KRK^{-1}$ anyway leaves an error (parallax) of about $\;f\,b/Z\;$ pixels for a sideways baseline $b$: the disparity of lecture 2.

Rule of thumb

Scene from $Z_{\min}$ to $\infty$: one $H$ is good when $\;f\,b / Z_{\min} \lesssim 1$ px (or the matching tolerance). A plane at depth $d$ instead, $H = K(R + \mathbf t\mathbf n^\top/d)K^{-1}$, leaves $\;f\,b\,\lvert 1/Z - 1/d\rvert$.

Examples

  • hand-held panorama with a phone: $f \approx 3000$ px, the lens moves $b \approx 2$ cm between shots: a building 60 m away 1 px, 20 m away 3 px, a person 2 m away 30 px (ghosting)
  • drone at $A = 100$ m, $f = 3650$ px, photos $b = 20$ m apart: the ground and the top of a 10 m tree differ by $3650 \cdot 20 \cdot (\tfrac{1}{90} - \tfrac{1}{100}) \approx 80$ px: not distant; the ground is a plane (case 1), trees and roofs break it (later)

Pure rotation: the homography is exact

Translation: parallax

wall H: the homography of the back wall, the plane $z = 20$ m in camera 1 ($\mathbf n = (0, 0, 1)$, $d = 20$): $\;H = K\big(R + \mathbf t\,\mathbf n^\top/d\big)K^{-1}$. Exact for the wall; nearer points shift more than it predicts and appear twice.
ground H: the same formula for the ground, the plane $y = 1$ m below the camera ($\mathbf n = (0, 1, 0)$, $d = 1$; $y$ points down). Now the ground aligns and the wall, far from that plane, is doubled.
With translation every plane has its own homography: no single $H$ fits a 3D scene. With pure rotation $\mathbf t = \mathbf 0$, the plane drops out and one $H = KRK^{-1}$ fits everything.

Estimating a homography: $H$ is the unknown

So far $H$ was given and we computed where a point goes. Now the points are known (correspondences $(u_i, v_i) \to (u'_i, v'_i)$) and the 9 entries of $H$ are the unknowns. As with the projection matrix $P$ in lecture 2, flatten $H$ into one vector:

$$H = \begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{11}} & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{12}} & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{13}}\\ \textcolor{#5fd38d}{h_{21}} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{h_{22}} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{h_{23}}\\ \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{31}} & \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{32}} & \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{33}}\end{bmatrix} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbf h = \begin{bmatrix}\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{11}\ \ h_{12}\ \ h_{13}}\ \ \textcolor{#5fd38d}{h_{21}\ \ h_{22}\ \ h_{23}}\ \ \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{31}\ \ h_{32}\ \ h_{33}}\end{bmatrix}^\top$$

One correspondence, the $u'$ equation; multiply out the denominator and collect the terms:

$$\begin{aligned} u' &= \frac{\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{11}}u + \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{12}}v + \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{13}}}{\textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{31}}u + \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{32}}v + \textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{33}}}\\ 0 &= u\,\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{11}} + v\,\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{12}} + 1\,\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{h_{13}} - u'u\,\textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{31}} - u'v\,\textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{32}} - u'\,\textcolor{#b28dff}{h_{33}}\end{aligned}$$

The numbers $u, v, u'$ are now coefficients: one linear equation in $\mathbf h$. With the $v'$ equation, two rows per correspondence:

$$\begin{bmatrix} u & v & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u'u & -u'v & -u'\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & u & v & 1 & -v'u & -v'v & -v'\end{bmatrix}\mathbf h = \mathbf 0$$

The roles swap

knownunknown
warping$H$, $(u, v)$$(u', v')$
estimating$(u_i, v_i)$, $(u'_i, v'_i)$$\mathbf h$ (9 numbers)

Why it is linear

$u'$ is a ratio of two expressions in $H$; cross-multiplying removes the ratio. The price: the equations are homogeneous, so $\mathbf h$ is found only up to scale, exactly as $H$ is defined.

Estimating a homography: solving for $\mathbf h$

Stack the two rows of every correspondence:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} u_1 & v_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u'_1u_1 & -u'_1v_1 & -u'_1\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & u_1 & v_1 & 1 & -v'_1u_1 & -v'_1v_1 & -v'_1\\ & & & & \vdots & & & & \\ u_n & v_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u'_nu_n & -u'_nv_n & -u'_n\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & u_n & v_n & 1 & -v'_nu_n & -v'_nv_n & -v'_n\end{bmatrix}}_{A:\ 2n \times 9}\mathbf h = \mathbf 0$$

  • $\mathbf h = \mathbf 0$ always solves it, and $\mathbf h$ is defined up to scale: $\;\min\|A\mathbf h\|$ s.t. $\|\mathbf h\| = 1$ (lecture 2)
  • solution: the right singular vector of $A$ for the smallest singular value
  • reshape $\mathbf h$ back into $H$: rows of three

How many points?

8 DoF, 2 equations per point → 4 points, no three of them collinear.

In practice

  • normalise first: shift the points to their centroid, scale to mean distance $\sqrt2$ (Hartley): pixel values $\sim 10^3$ next to $1$ make $A$ badly conditioned
  • refine by minimising the reprojection error in pixels
  • with wrong matches: RANSAC with $s = 4$
  • cv2.findHomography(src, dst, cv2.RANSAC, 3.0)

Composing the homographies

Homographies compose by matrix products: $H_{1\to3} = H_{2\to3}\,H_{1\to2}$ (first $1 \to 2$, then $2 \to 3$), and $H_{4\to3} = H_{3\to4}^{-1}$. The canvas adds a translation $T$ so that all coordinates are positive: photo $i$ is warped by $T\,H_{i\to3}$.

Errors multiply along the chain: the outer photos are the least accurate. Choosing the middle photo as the reference keeps the chains short and the stretching symmetric.

Warping into a common frame

  • pick a reference image (the middle one): the others are warped into its frame
  • compose the pairwise homographies into $H_{i \to \text{ref}}$ (previous slide)
  • size of the result (the canvas): warp the four corners of every image; their bounding box $[x_{\min}, x_{\max}] \times [y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$ is in the reference frame, and $x_{\min}, y_{\min} < 0$ (photos left of / above the reference). Canvas pixels start at $(0, 0)$, so shift by $T = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0 & -x_{\min}\\ 0 & 1 & -y_{\min}\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$: photo $i$ is warped by $T H_{i \to \text{ref}}$
  • backward warping (lecture 1): for every output pixel, $H^{-1}$ gives the source position; interpolate (bilinear)

Many images

  • chaining accumulates errors: the last image drifts (the same problem as odometry, Part 4)
  • remedy: bundle adjustment: adjust all rotations (and $f$) together so that all matches agree (Brown & Lowe, AutoStitch, 2007)
  • wide panoramas (> ~120°) stretch without bound on one plane: project onto a cylinder or a sphere instead

Warping onto the reference plane, in 3D

One camera centre, three photos (small frames) taken by turning it. Every pixel of photo $i$ defines a ray; the panorama pixel is where the ray meets the image plane of the reference photo, extended.

This is exactly the homography $H = KR_{\text{ref}}R_i^\top K^{-1}$: the same rays, a different plane.

Turn the outer photos further: their projections stretch, and at 90° their rays run parallel to the plane and never meet it.

Warping and blending to play with

panorama: “Canary Wharf”, Andreas Mischok, Poly Haven (CC0)

Blending

The images differ in exposure, vignetting and white balance; small misalignments remain. Overwriting gives visible seams.

Feathering: a weighted average, weights falling off towards each image's border:

$$I(\mathbf x) = \frac{\sum_i w_i(\mathbf x)\, I_i(\mathbf x)}{\sum_i w_i(\mathbf x)}$$

$w_i(\mathbf x)$: distance from $\mathbf x$ to the border of image $i$: a distance transform of the image's mask (cv2.distanceTransform).

Better blending

  • exposure compensation: one gain per image, fitted on the overlaps
  • seam finding: cut where the images agree (graph cut): hides parallax and moving people
  • multi-band blending (Burt & Adelson, 1983): blend low frequencies over a wide band, details over a narrow one: no ghosting, no seams

All of it: cv2.Stitcher_create().

Homographies in robotics

  • bird's-eye view of the floor or the road (inverse perspective mapping): the ground is a plane, so camera → top view is one homography, from 4 marked floor points; parking assistants, lane detection, mobile robots
  • aerial mosaics: a drone far above the ground (cases 1 and 3), maps for agriculture and inspection. For flat ground this is orthorectification (orthophotos, orthomosaics); hilly terrain needs a terrain model instead of one homography
  • planar markers: the 4 corners of an ArUco/AprilTag give a homography; the pose follows from $H = K[\mathbf r_1\ \mathbf r_2\ \mathbf t]$ (lecture 2, IPPE)
  • calibration: Zhang's method, one homography per view of the board (lecture 2)
  • stereo rectification: one homography per image makes the epipolar lines horizontal (lecture 2 → Part 3)
  • image stabilisation: undo the camera's small rotations between frames with $KRK^{-1}$ (the rotation from a gyroscope)

Bird's-eye view: one homography of the floor

The floor is a plane, so camera image → top view is a single homography: here from the camera's height and pitch, or from 4 marked floor points. The floor comes out metric (the marked square is a square, distances can be read off the grid). Everything above the floor (box, sphere) is smeared away from the camera: the homography holds only for points on that plane.

Aerial mosaics: photogrammetry flights

The flight

  • a drone flies parallel lines back and forth (a "lawnmower" pattern), camera pointing straight down (nadir)
  • overlap: about 75 to 80% along a line, 60 to 70% between lines: every ground point is in several photos
  • ground sampling distance (the size of one pixel on the ground): $\;\text{GSD} = A / f$, with $A$ the altitude and $f$ in pixels. Example: $f \approx 3650$ px (1-inch sensor, 8.8 mm lens), $A = 100$ m → 2.7 cm/px

Flat ground: homographies

  • photo $i$ ↔ ground plane: $H_i = K[\mathbf r_1\ \mathbf r_2\ \mathbf t]$ (case 1), the pose from GPS + IMU
  • refined with feature matches between overlapping photos and bundle adjustment; ground control points or RTK GPS give centimetre accuracy

Real terrain: relief displacement

  • a point at height $h$ maps through the ground homography as if it were on the ground, shifted away from the image centre by $\;\Delta r = r\,h / A$ ($r$: its distance from the centre in the image)
  • buildings and trees lean outwards, differently in each photo: the mosaic breaks there
  • the real pipeline (next lecture): structure from motion → 3D points → surface model → a true orthophoto, every point seen straight from above; OpenDroneMap (open source), Pix4D, Metashape

Every new photo: its pose gives $H_i = K[\mathbf r_1\ \mathbf r_2\ \mathbf t]$ (readout), each map pixel is sent through $H_i$ into the photo (backward warping), and the photos are feathered. Westbound photos are turned by 180°: $H_i$ contains that rotation. Exact poses here; real GPS/IMU poses: next slide.

A good keypoint is

  • repeatable: the same physical point is found in both images
  • well localised: its position is precise in both directions
  • distinctive: its neighbourhood looks unlike others

…despite

  • translation, rotation, scale (the robot moves closer)
  • brightness and contrast changes
  • moderate changes of viewpoint, noise, blur

and fast enough for a robot: hundreds to thousands of points per frame, at frame rate.

What is a good keypoint?

The experiment: take the small square window (orange) around a point, shift it by $(\Delta u, \Delta v)$ and measure how different it looks from the unshifted window: $E(\Delta) = \sum_W \big(I(\mathbf x + \Delta) - I(\mathbf x)\big)^2$. A point is easy to find again if every shift makes a difference.

shift error: $E$ for all shifts within ±8 px, the centre is no shift. Dark: looks the same, bright: different. flat: dark everywhere (no shift is noticed). edge: a dark valley along the edge (sliding along it is not noticed). corner: dark only at the centre (every shift is noticed).
quadratic: for small shifts $E(\Delta) \approx \Delta^\top M \Delta$, a $2\times2$ matrix computed from the image gradients (next slide). Its ellipse summarises the error surface; the semi-axes show the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2$.
eigenvalues of $M$: both small: flat; one large: edge; both large: corner. "Harris corners" marks every point of the image that passes this test.

Harris corner detector

Formally the image is a differentiable function $I: \mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R$ (the brightness before sampling, or a smooth interpolation of the pixels); the pixels are its samples. $I_x = \partial I/\partial x$, $I_y = \partial I/\partial y$: its partial derivatives, at the pixels approximated by finite differences (e.g. Sobel). The gradient $\nabla I(\mathbf x) = \begin{bmatrix} I_x(\mathbf x)\\ I_y(\mathbf x)\end{bmatrix}$ is, at each pixel, a column vector in $\mathbb R^2$: so $\nabla I^\top\Delta$ is a number and $\nabla I\,\nabla I^\top$ a $2\times2$ matrix.

Shift the window by $\Delta \in \mathbb R^2$; Taylor: $I(\mathbf x + \Delta) \approx I(\mathbf x) + \nabla I(\mathbf x)^\top \Delta$

$$\begin{aligned} E(\Delta) &= \sum_{\mathbf x \in W} \big(I(\mathbf x + \Delta) - I(\mathbf x)\big)^2 \approx \sum_{\mathbf x \in W} \big(\nabla I(\mathbf x)^\top \Delta\big)^2\\ &= \sum_{\mathbf x \in W} \Delta^\top\, \nabla I(\mathbf x)\,\nabla I(\mathbf x)^\top\, \Delta = \Delta^\top M\, \Delta \end{aligned}$$

$$M = \sum_{\mathbf x \in W} \nabla I\,\nabla I^\top = \sum_{\mathbf x \in W}\begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y\\ I_x I_y & I_y^2\end{bmatrix}$$

The structure tensor: a quadratic form again; its eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2$ say how fast $E$ grows in the best and worst direction.

Harris & Stephens (1988), no eigenvalues needed:

$$R = \det M - k\,(\operatorname{tr} M)^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2 - k(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^2$$

$k \approx 0.04$ to $0.06$; keypoints = local maxima of $R$ above a threshold. Shi-Tomasi: $\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$, cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack.

Scale

Whether a structure is a corner depends on the window size relative to the image of it: which changes when the robot moves closer.

Scale space: blur the image at many scales $\sigma$ (Gaussian pyramid); look for extrema in $(x, y, \sigma)$ of a blob response, e.g. the difference of Gaussians (SIFT). Each keypoint gets a position and a characteristic scale.

Orientation: the dominant gradient direction around the point. Describing the patch in this scaled, rotated frame makes the descriptor invariant to scale and rotation.

Descriptors: SIFT optional

→ a 128-dimensional vector, normalised to unit length

SIFT (Lowe, 1999/2004) turns the neighbourhood of a keypoint into 128 numbers, in four steps:

  1. the patch: 16×16 samples around the keypoint, sized by its scale and turned by its orientation (previous slide), so both images give the same physical patch
  2. gradients (blue arrows): at each sample, the direction and strength of the brightness change; a brightness offset does not change them
  3. histograms: split the patch into 4×4 cells; in each cell, every arrow adds its length to one of 8 direction bins (orange stars: the length of each ray is the bin). An edge that moves by a pixel stays in its cell: robust to small shifts and deformations
  4. the vector: 16 cells × 8 bins = 128 numbers, normalised to unit length: robust to contrast ($aI$ multiplies all gradients by $a$)

Two descriptors are compared by their Euclidean distance. "Detect, describe, match" used the same idea with 2×2 cells (32 numbers).

Descriptors: ORB optional

ORB (Rublee et al., 2011) = oriented FAST + rotated BRIEF: FAST on an image pyramid (scale), the best corners by the Harris score; an orientation from the patch's brightness centroid, and the pairs are turned by it (photo 2 is turned by 4°). Only comparisons, 32 bytes per point, distance by XOR and popcount: one to two orders of magnitude faster than SIFT. The feature of ORB-SLAM.

photo: crop of “Canary Wharf”, Andreas Mischok, Poly Haven (CC0)

Matching

  • for each descriptor in image 1: the nearest descriptor in image 2
  • repeated structures (windows, tiles) have several near-identical neighbours → ratio test (Lowe): accept only if
    $$\frac{d_1}{d_2} < 0.8$$
    $d_1, d_2$: distances to the nearest and the second nearest
  • mutual check: also the nearest in the other direction
  • then RANSAC on the geometry (a homography, or $F$ in Part 3)

cv2.BFMatcher().knnMatch(d1, d2, k=2); many points: approximate nearest neighbours (FLANN, FAISS).

Part 3

Two-view geometry

a general pair of cameras, a general scene

The problem

Given: two images of a 3D scene from a camera that moved (rotated and translated); matched features; usually $K$.

Find: the relative pose $R, \mathbf t$ between the two views (the camera as a motion sensor), and then the 3D points.

No homography relates the images (parallax). Do the matches constrain anything?

Compared with lecture 2

  • PnP: known 3D points → pose. Here: no 3D points.
  • rectified stereo: known pose, the match lies on the same row. Here: unknown pose, and the match lies on a line: the epipolar line.

Epipolar geometry: the terms

Two cameras: centres $O_l$, $O_r$, and their image planes.

The baseline joins the centres. Where it pierces the image planes: the epipoles $\mathbf e_l$, $\mathbf e_r$. Equivalently, $\mathbf e_r$ is the image of the left camera's centre in the right camera, and vice versa.

A scene point $P$ and the two centres span a plane: the epipolar plane of $P$. Both rays, and both pixels $\mathbf p_l$, $\mathbf p_r$, lie in it.

The plane cuts the images in the epipolar lines $\mathbf l_l$, $\mathbf l_r$. Wherever $P$ is on the ray of $\mathbf p_l$ (unknown depth), its image lies on $\mathbf l_r$: the match of $\mathbf p_l$ is on one line.

Every epipolar plane contains the baseline (the planes turn about it like the pages of a book), so every epipolar line passes through the epipole.

Epipolar geometry in 3D

Why it matters: matching becomes a 1D search along the line, and wrong matches can be rejected; the line depends only on the relative pose $R, \mathbf t$ (and $K$), so every correct match is a measurement of the camera motion (Part 4).

The epipolar constraint

Point in the left camera frame $\mathbf x_l$, in the right frame $\mathbf x_r = R\,\mathbf x_l + \mathbf t$ (all vectors in the right camera's axes):

  • $\mathbf t$: from $O_r$ to $O_l$ (put $\mathbf x_l = \mathbf 0$)
  • $\mathbf x_r$: from $O_r$ to $P$; $\;R\mathbf x_l = \mathbf x_r - \mathbf t$: from $O_l$ to $P$

All three lie in the epipolar plane. $\mathbf t \times R\mathbf x_l$ is perpendicular to both $\mathbf t$ and $R\mathbf x_l$, so it is the normal of the epipolar plane, and $\mathbf x_r$, lying in the plane, is perpendicular to it:

$$\mathbf x_r^\top\,(\mathbf t \times R\,\mathbf x_l) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf x_r^\top\, \underbrace{[\mathbf t]_\times R}_{E}\, \mathbf x_l = 0$$

$$[\mathbf t]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -t_z & t_y\\ t_z & 0 & -t_x\\ -t_y & t_x & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad [\mathbf t]_\times \mathbf a = \mathbf t \times \mathbf a$$

The essential matrix $E = [\mathbf t]_\times R$

$$\mathbf x_r^\top E\, \mathbf x_l = 0$$
  • no depth needed: we know only the pixel, not the 3D point $\mathbf x_l$. But replacing $\mathbf x_l$ by $\lambda\mathbf x_l$ multiplies the left side by $\lambda$, and $0$ stays $0$. So any point on the ray will do, e.g. the one at depth 1: $\hat{\mathbf x}_l = K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_l = (x_l/z_l,\ y_l/z_l,\ 1)$; the same for $\mathbf x_r$: $\;\hat{\mathbf x}_r^\top E\,\hat{\mathbf x}_l = 0$
  • $E\mathbf x_l$ is the epipolar line in the right (normalised) image: one equation, one line
  • $E$ is defined up to scale; $\det E = 0$ (rank 2)
  • 5 DoF: 3 (rotation) + 3 (translation) − 1 (scale)

Example: rectified stereo

Right camera at $(b, 0, 0)$, same orientation: $R = I$, $\mathbf t = (-b, 0, 0)$.

$$E = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & b\\ 0 & -b & 0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf x_r^\top E\,\mathbf x_l = b\,(y_r z_l - z_r y_l) = 0$$

$\Rightarrow\; y_r / z_r = y_l / z_l$: the same row, as in lecture 2.

Pixels: the fundamental matrix $F$

Pixels: $\tilde{\mathbf u}_l \equiv K_l\mathbf x_l$, $\tilde{\mathbf u}_r \equiv K_r\mathbf x_r$. Substitute $\mathbf x_l \equiv K_l^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_l$, $\mathbf x_r \equiv K_r^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}_r$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf u}_r^\top \underbrace{K_r^{-\top} E\, K_l^{-1}}_{F}\, \tilde{\mathbf u}_l = 0$$

  • $F$ needs no calibration: estimated from pixel matches alone
  • 7 DoF: up to scale, $\det F = 0$
  • epipolar line in the right image: $\mathbf l_r = F\tilde{\mathbf u}_l$, i.e. $l_1 u + l_2 v + l_3 = 0$
  • epipoles: $F\,\tilde{\mathbf e}_l = \mathbf 0$, $\;F^\top\tilde{\mathbf e}_r = \mathbf 0$ (every epipolar line passes through them)

Known $K$ (a robot's camera)

$E = K_r^\top F K_l$: estimate $E$ directly from normalised points. Fewer DoF (5), more stable, and $E$ contains the pose.

Unknown $K$ (photos from the internet)

$F$ only; the reconstruction is then determined up to a projective transformation (calibration adds the metric structure).

Estimating $F$: the eight-point algorithm

Each match $(u_l, v_l) \leftrightarrow (u_r, v_r)$ gives one equation, linear in the entries of $F$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} u_r u_l & u_r v_l & u_r & v_r u_l & v_r v_l & v_r & u_l & v_l & 1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf f = 0$$

  1. stack $n \ge 8$ matches: $A\mathbf f = \mathbf 0$, $\min\|A\mathbf f\|$ s.t. $\|\mathbf f\| = 1$ (third time today)
  2. enforce rank 2: SVD $F = U\Sigma V^\top$, set $\sigma_3 = 0$
  3. normalise the pixel coordinates first (Hartley, "In defense of the eight-point algorithm", 1997)

With outliers and known $K$

  • RANSAC with the minimal solver: 7 points for $F$, 5 points for $E$ (Nistér, 2004)
  • inlier test: distance of $\tilde{\mathbf u}_r$ from the line $F\tilde{\mathbf u}_l$ (Sampson distance)
  • cv2.findFundamentalMat(p1, p2, cv2.FM_RANSAC)
    cv2.findEssentialMat(p1, p2, K, cv2.RANSAC)

With 50% outliers ($p = 0.99$): $s = 8$ needs 1177 iterations, $s = 5$ only 146.

From $E$ to the pose optional

SVD: $\;E = U\,\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)\,V^\top$ (with $\det U = \det V = +1$), and

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{aligned} R &= U W V^\top \ \text{ or } \ U W^\top V^\top\\ \mathbf t &= \pm\,\mathbf u_3\end{aligned}$$

$\mathbf u_3$: the third column of $U$ (the null vector of $E^\top$).

  • so $E$ gives $\mathbf t$ (up to sign and scale) and two possible $R$: four candidate poses
  • only one puts the triangulated points in front of both cameras (positive depth, "cheirality")
  • as with P3P's four solutions (lecture 2): an extra check picks the right one

cv2.recoverPose(E, p1, p2, K) → $R$, $\mathbf t$ with $\|\mathbf t\| = 1$.

Triangulation

Known $P_l = K[I\,|\,\mathbf 0]$, $P_r = K[R\,|\,\mathbf t]$ and a match: intersect the two rays. With noise they miss each other:

  • DLT: $\tilde{\mathbf u} \times P\tilde{\mathbf X} = \mathbf 0$, two equations per view, $A\tilde{\mathbf X} = \mathbf 0$ (fourth time)
  • better: minimise the reprojection error
  • poor when the rays are almost parallel (small baseline, far point): lecture 2, $\Delta z \sim z^2$

cv2.triangulatePoints(P1, P2, p1, p2)

The missing scale

Where the scale comes from

  • a stereo pair: the known baseline (lecture 2)
  • an IMU (visual-inertial odometry: accelerations are metric)
  • wheel odometry, a known object size, a depth sensor

Scale the scene and the camera motion by the same factor $k$: every ray keeps its direction, the images are identical.

  • $E$ (and the pose from it) is known only up to scale: $\|\mathbf t\| = 1$ by convention
  • a single moving camera cannot tell a toy house 1 m away from a real one 20 m away

Film makers rely on it

Miniatures: spaceships, cities and disasters were filmed as models with a small camera moving slowly (motion control), and the audience could not tell. The "bigatures" of The Lord of the Rings: Minas Tirith at 1/72 scale. What gives a miniature away is not geometry but physics and optics (water, fire, falling objects, a shallow depth of field), so miniatures are filmed at a higher frame rate, about $\sqrt{1/\text{scale}}$ times normal, and played back slowed down.

Why odometry is not enough: drift

From odometry to SLAM

SLAM: simultaneous localisation and mapping. Keep a map, and use it:

  • loop closure: recognise a place seen before (bag of visual words, learned place recognition), add a constraint "this pose ≈ that pose"
  • optimise the whole trajectory (pose graph) or the poses and the points together (bundle adjustment)
  • relocalise after losing track (PnP against the map)

Systems

  • ORB-SLAM3 (2021): features, mono / stereo / RGB-D, with an IMU
  • VINS-Mono (2018): visual-inertial
  • DROID-SLAM (2021): learned, dense
  • MASt3R-SLAM (2025): on top of a 3D foundation model

Summary

  • a homography relates two images of a plane, or two images taken by a rotating camera: $H = KRK^{-1}$; otherwise parallax
  • $H$: 8 DoF, 4 points, DLT + RANSAC; stitching = warp into a reference frame + blend
  • features: repeatable, well localised, distinctive; Harris ($M$, two large eigenvalues), SIFT, ORB; ratio test; learned features and matchers
  • general pair: the match lies on the epipolar line; $\mathbf x_r^\top E\,\mathbf x_l = 0$, $E = [\mathbf t]_\times R$ (5 DoF), $F = K_r^{-\top}EK_l^{-1}$ (7 DoF)
  • 8-point / 5-point + RANSAC → $E$ → four poses, cheirality picks one; triangulation
  • monocular: no scale; visual odometry drifts → loop closure, SLAM
One recipe all along: write the model as linear equations, solve $\min\|A\mathbf x\|$ with $\|\mathbf x\| = 1$, wrap it in RANSAC, refine the error in pixels.

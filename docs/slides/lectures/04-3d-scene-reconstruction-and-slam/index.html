Why many views?

One point, seen from $N$ cameras. Every observation is noisy*, so the estimated rays do not meet perfectly. We use least-squares to find the (approximate) solution.

  • more views: the uncertainty shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$
  • a wider baseline helps much more: the depth uncertainty is $\propto Z^2 / B$ (lecture 2) - intuition: rays that are parallel don't help
  • every view also constrains the cameras: with enough points in common, the poses and even the intrinsics follow from the photos alone (self-calibration)
  • coverage: parts hidden in one photo are seen in another; wrong matches are outvoted

* Some uncertainty is inevitable: a feature is located to about half a pixel at best, and which pixel a photon lands in is random.

Correspondence search

  1. Feature extraction → in COLMAP it is SIFT (lecture 3)
  2. Matching → images with many matched features form a potential pair
  3. Geometric verification → for each potential pair, estimate the transformation between the two images; if enough matches agree with it, the pair is verified
At the end we have pairs of images, and for each pair the geometric relation between its two images.

Constraints: 2 × 1 k × 10 k = 20 M (pixel coordinates × #features per image × #images)

Question: how does the general formulation map to our case?
How the general nonlinear formulation f(A, b) = u maps to our case? If we estimate everything, A is empty. If one knows the camera calibration parameters, A contains exactly these parameters. Example: 10 drones flying around a scene and taking pictures. For each observation: inputs (A): camera parameters such as the intrinsic matrix or the distortion coefficients that were used to generate a given pixel value; outputs (u): the observed pixel values; unknowns (b): the 3D point positions and the camera poses. Many values in A repeat (whenever the same drone produced the pixel), but these are our input observations.

Linear regression: least squares

This is least squares, i.e. the closest distance!
$$A\cdot\mathbf b = [\mathbf a_1, \ldots, \mathbf a_k]\cdot\mathbf b = \mathbf u$$
the columns span a subspace; $\mathbf b$ picks a combination of them; with more constraints than columns, $\mathbf u$ is usually not in the subspace
$$d(\mathbf b) = \|A\mathbf b - \mathbf u\|^2$$
we can't get $\mathbf u$, so the next best thing is the closest point in the subspace, i.e. minimise the (squared) distance
$$d(\mathbf b + \mathbf h) - d(\mathbf b) = \htmlClass{mark}{2\,(A\mathbf b - \mathbf u)^\top A\,\mathbf h} + \mathbf h^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf h$$
linear term → the first derivative
$$(A\mathbf b - \mathbf u)^\top A = 0 \iff \htmlClass{mark}{A^\top A}\,\mathbf b = A^\top\mathbf u$$
$A^\top A$: squared volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the columns of $A$ → invertible if the columns are linearly independent
$$\mathbf b = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf u$$
with these coefficients we get the desired projection

Bundle adjustment to play with (in 2D)

A flat world: cameras with a 1D image (one pixel coordinate per observation), points in the plane. The estimate starts perturbed; every step linearises, solves and moves all cameras and points at once, and the residuals (the ticks on the 1D images) shrink together. Camera 0 and the distance to camera 1 are held fixed: the images do not say where the whole model is or how big it is.

SLAM: the problem

A robot in an unknown environment needs its pose (to control itself) and a map (to plan). Each helps with the other:

SLAM: neither is known. Estimate the poses and the map together, from noisy motion and noisy observations.

Dead reckoning: the robot without a map

Dead reckoning adds up the odometry. Each step adds a small, independent random error, so the estimate performs a random walk: its spread grows like $\sqrt{\text{number of steps}}$. An error in the estimated direction of travel (the heading $\theta$) impacts also position estimates: every later step is added in a wrong direction, so the error grows with the distance driven. And if the chain breaks (the robot is carried, the sensor is covered), it is lost for good: with no map, there is nothing to find itself with.

SLAM, mathematically

Unknown too, but we already have an estimate of it from the previous step. Both poses keep being adjusted.
$$\htmlClass{t-odo}{\mathbf z_t} = \htmlClass{t-f}{f}\big(\htmlClass{t-xp pose}{\mathbf x_{t-1}}, \htmlClass{t-x pose}{\mathbf x_t}\big)$$
Measured: the robot's motion since the last step (noisy)
e.g. from the images (visual odometry*), or from the wheels
the motion that the two poses imply
Unknown: the robot's pose at step $t$: where it is and which way it faces
$$\htmlClass{t-obs}{\mathbf z_{t,k}} = \htmlClass{t-h}{h}\big(\htmlClass{pose}{\mathbf x_t}, \htmlClass{t-l lm}{\mathbf l_k}\big)$$
Measured: the distance (range) and the direction (bearing) to landmark $k$ (noisy)
e.g. from a laser scanner; a camera gives the pixel instead
what the sensor should see from $\mathbf x_t$ if the landmark is at $\mathbf l_k$
Unknown: where landmark $k$ is
With a camera as the sensor, this is Part 1 again: the landmarks are 3D points, $\mathbf z_{t,k}$ is the pixel where point $k$ appears in image $t$, and $h$ is the projection $\pi$. SLAM is bundle adjustment running on the robot.

* odometry: from Greek hodós, road, and métron, measure

Can't solve directly so we define the cost function

$$\min_{\mathbf x_{0:T},\ \mathbf l_{1:K}}\ \ \htmlClass{t-prior}{\frac{\big\|\mathbf x_0 - \bar{\mathbf x}_0\big\|^2}{\sigma_0^2}} \;+\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \htmlClass{t-odot}{\frac{\big\|f(\mathbf x_{t-1}, \mathbf x_t) - \mathbf z_t\big\|^2}{\sigma_{\text{odo}}^2}} \;+\; \sum_{(t,k)} \htmlClass{t-obst}{\frac{\big\|h(\mathbf x_t, \mathbf l_k) - \mathbf z_{t,k}\big\|^2}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}^2}}$$
prior: estimated starting pose is what we measured at the beginning
odometry: consecutive poses should agree with the measured motion between them
observations: every landmark should be where the robot saw it from
weights $1/\sigma^2$: a measurement counts according to its noise; when measurements disagree, the precise ones win over the noisy ones.
The same recipe as bundle adjustment: for every measurement, (what it should be, given the unknowns) − (what it was), squared, weighted, summed. Then linearise, solve, move, iterate.

Where the cost comes from

$$\htmlClass{t-e}{e_i} = z_i - h_i(\mathbf x) \;\sim\; \htmlClass{t-N}{\mathcal N(0, \sigma_i^2)}$$
the error of measurement $i$: what it was, minus what it should be given the unknowns $\mathbf x$
Assumption: the noise is Gaussian: usually small, rarely large; $\sigma_i$ says how noisy
$$\htmlClass{t-p}{p}(e_1, \ldots, e_n \mid \mathbf x) = \prod_i \htmlClass{t-exp}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_i}\,\exp\Big(-\frac{e_i^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\Big)}$$
how likely are the errors we see, given $\mathbf x$? The best $\mathbf x$ makes them most likely
independent measurements: a product of bell curves
$$\arg\max_{\mathbf x}\ p \;=\; \arg\min_{\mathbf x}\ \sum_i \htmlClass{t-w}{\frac{e_i^2}{\sigma_i^2}}$$
the log turns the product into a sum and the max into a min; the constants drop out
the weights $1/\sigma^2$ fall out by themselves: a precise measurement counts more
Least squares with $1/\sigma^2$ weights is the most likely explanation of the measurements, if their noise is Gaussian. That is where the cost comes from.

Factor graphs: a picture of the cost

  • circles: the unknowns (poses, landmarks); squares: the terms of the cost (prior, odometry, observations)
  • a square touches only the variables in its term: the graph is the sparsity pattern, the same zeros as in bundle adjustment
  • adding a measurement = adding a square; a loop closure = a square between variables far apart in time: a landmark seen again (top), or the motion measured directly between two poses (bottom, as in the animation on the loop-closure slide)

SLAM to play with

The whole graph is re-optimised at every step. Watch the ellipses: the pose uncertainty grows along the lap, the landmarks inherit it, and when the first landmarks are seen again the loop closes: everything moves and the ellipses collapse.

Loop closure

  • odometry and tracking are local: their errors add up along the way (the drift)
  • recognising a place seen before gives one more measurement, between now and then
  • the optimiser spreads the error around the loop: the whole lap is corrected at once
  • a wrong loop closure distorts the entire map, so it has to be well verified, like any match (e.g. RANSAC, lecture 3)

A front end per sensor, one optimiser: every measurement adds its own terms, with its own $\sigma$, to the same cost.

Non-RGB based SLAM example

  • a LiDAR or a depth camera gives a cloud of points; the next scan gives another cloud, from a slightly different pose
  • pair every point with the closest point of the other scan; find the rotation and translation that align the pairs best (least squares again, this one even has a closed form); move the scan and repeat: as the scans get closer, the pairs get better
  • it converges only from a close enough start: the previous pose, or the odometry, provides it
The result is the motion between the two scans: an odometry term $\mathbf z_t$, or a loop closure, for the SLAM cost.

Iterative closest point (ICP), Besl & McKay 1992.

Summary

  • SfM: features → matched pairs → geometric verification; then one image at a time: PnP, triangulation and bundle adjustment (COLMAP)
  • bundle adjustment: minimise the reprojection error over all cameras and points at once: more constraints than unknowns, so least squares; nonlinear, so linearise, solve, move, iterate; the matrix is sparse
  • NeRFs and Gaussian splatting build on the cameras and points that structure from motion gives
  • SLAM: the same least squares on a moving robot: unknowns = poses + landmarks; prior + odometry + observation terms, each weighted by its noise
  • a loop closure is one more measurement between now and then: it corrects the whole lap at once
  • a front end per sensor turns data into measurements; one back end optimises; keyframes keep it real-time
Write down what each measurement should be given the unknowns, subtract what it was, square, weight, sum; then linearise and solve, over a sparse graph. Reconstruction and SLAM are both instances of this.

Part 1

Two kinds of flying robots

Flying an RC plane: four inputs

The pilot sets only thrust and three control surfaces: ailerons, elevator, rudder. Everything else is done by the air.

What each control surface does

A deflected surface changes the lift on its part of the plane: ailerons in opposite directions → roll, elevator → pitch, rudder → yaw, throttle → airspeed. Holding a deflection gives a rotation rate, not an angle.

A plane stabilizes itself

Sticks centred, a gust hits: the tail, the fin and the wing dihedral turn the plane back into straight flight, with no electronics. Passive stability is designed into the shape; remember this when we get to multicopters.

A multicopter: try to fly it motor by motor

  • no passive stability: a 2% thrust difference tips it over in about a second, and the tilt then pushes it sideways
  • holding it level takes corrections every few milliseconds (flight controllers: 0.4 to 8 kHz); a human reacts in about 0.2 s
  • every motor changes thrust, roll, pitch and yaw at once
  • 1907, 1920s: four-rotor machines lifted off but were very hard to control; the mechanically stabilized helicopter won
  • quadcopters returned once the electronics were cheap: MEMS gyros and accelerometers, fast microcontrollers, brushless motors, LiPo batteries
  • around 2010 hobby boards (MultiWii, with the Wii MotionPlus gyro), Parrot AR.Drone (2010), DJI Phantom (2013)

Fixed-wing and multicopter: hard, yet everywhere

fixed-wing: lift from airspeed

  • wings lift while air flows over them; the motor only keeps the airspeed up
  • stable by design: it glides when the motor stops
  • efficient and fast, but needs a runway (or a catapult) and cannot stop in the air

multicopter: lift from rotor thrust

  • hard: every motor does thrust and attitude at once; no passive stability, controller off = crash; underactuated; all lift paid for with battery power
  • everywhere: hovers, takes off vertically, positions precisely, four motors and no linkages; the difficulty is moved into software

Other multicopters: hexa- and octocopters (a motor may fail, Part 2), coaxial pairs; hybrids (VTOL planes) take off like a copter and cruise like a plane.

Part 2

Anatomy and actuation

Quadcopter anatomy

  • ESC (electronic speed controller): turns the flight controller's command into the three-phase current of one brushless motor
  • flight controller: a microcontroller with the IMU (gyroscope + accelerometer) and a barometer on the same board
  • the compass sits on a mast with the GPS: far from the motor currents (Part 4)
  • separate radio links for control (receiver) and for video

Two real quadcopters: what is where

Body frame and the four commands

commandwhat it changes
throttle $T$total thrust: climb or descend
roll $R$bank left / right, about $x$
pitch $P$nose up / down, about $y$
yaw $Y$turn the nose, about $z$

Aviation convention FRD (forward, right, down), as in ArduPilot and PX4; the world frame is NED (north, east, down). Positive roll = right side down, positive pitch = nose up, positive yaw = nose to the right.

Roll, pitch, yaw: which is which

a ship rolls from side to side in the waves; a barrel rolla ship pitches its bow up and down over a wavea weather vane yaws into the wind; the nose swings left and right
a cat rolls over on its backa pitcher tips forward to poura door yaws on its vertical hinge

Same order everywhere: roll, pitch, yaw ↔ x, y, z ↔ R, G, B colours ↔ φ, θ, ψ. Sign: right-hand rule, thumb along the axis.

Four motors, six degrees of freedom: tilt to translate

  • every rotor pushes along the body "up" axis: the vehicle can only change its total thrust and the three torques
  • 4 inputs, 6 degrees of freedom (3 position, 3 orientation): underactuated
  • to go forward, first pitch the nose down; the thrust gets a horizontal component
  • to keep the altitude while tilted, the thrust must grow: $T\cos\theta = mg$

So position control works through attitude control: the position controller asks for an attitude.

Thrust grows with $\omega^2$: hover at half of the maximum

  • a propeller throws air down: thrust $T = k_T\,\omega^2$, and its drag torque on the motor $Q = k_Q\,\omega^2$ (the yaw slides that follow)
  • power $\propto \omega^3 \propto T^{3/2}$: doubling the thrust (hover → full) takes $2^{3/2} \approx 2.8\times$ the power (0.35 → 1 in the plot)
  • rule of thumb: thrust-to-weight about 2:1, so the vehicle hovers at half of the maximum thrust (71% of the motor speed)

why keep half of the thrust in reserve?

to climb; to tilt without losing altitude ($T\cos\theta = mg$: up to 60° at 2:1); to make torques, where one motor speeds up while another slows down; against wind and a sagging battery voltage. Racing quads have 5:1 to 10:1.

Flight time: a bigger battery is not always better

$$P = \frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A}}\cdot\frac{1}{\mathrm{FM}\cdot\eta}, \qquad t = \frac{m_{\text{batt}}\,e}{P}$$

Momentum theory: $A$ = total disk area of the rotors, $\rho$ = air density, figure of merit FM ≈ 0.6 (real vs ideal rotor), motor and ESC efficiency $\eta$ ≈ 0.8, $e$ = usable energy per kg. A heavier battery must lift itself: $t \propto m_b/(m_f+m_b)^{3/2}$ peaks at $m_b = 2m_f$, usually beyond the 2:1 limit. Larger propellers (larger $A$) need less power.

A spinning propeller also twists the frame

  • the motor drives the propeller against the air drag; the propeller drives the motor (and the frame) back the other way
  • a propeller spinning clockwise (seen from above) twists the frame counter-clockwise
  • helicopter: a tail rotor cancels the main rotor's torque
  • multicopter: half the rotors spin each way, and the difference is used to steer the yaw

To tilt forward, slow down the front motors

A multicopter has no control surfaces: every rotation comes from different motor speeds. Front slower and rear faster pitches the nose down; the tilted thrust then pulls the drone forward. Tilting right: left motors faster, right motors slower.

X frame or + frame?

Same motors, same arms, same limit on each motor: the X frame tilts faster (√2 more torque), forward and sideways look the same to the pilot, and the camera sees between the propellers. The + frame steers each axis with one pair only. Almost every multicopter today is an X.

Which motor spins which way?

Pitch command: front motors slower, rear motors faster (or the reverse). Roll command: left and right. In layout 3 every such pair has one CW and one CCW motor on each side, so the reaction torques of the changed motors cancel; yaw is then a separate command: speed up one diagonal, slow down the other.

Motor mixing: ArduPilot QUAD/X

$$\begin{bmatrix} m_1\\ m_2\\ m_3\\ m_4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{-1} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{+1} & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{+1}\\ 1 & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{+1} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{-1} & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{+1}\\ 1 & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{+1} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{+1} & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{-1}\\ 1 & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{-1} & \textcolor{#5fd38d}{-1} & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{-1}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T\\ \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{R}\\ \textcolor{#5fd38d}{P}\\ \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{Y}\end{bmatrix}$$

ArduCopter QUAD/X: motor 1 front-right CCW, 2 rear-left CCW, 3 front-left CW, 4 rear-right CW (seen from above). FRD frame: roll right, nose up, nose right are positive.

  • roll right: left motors (2, 3) faster, right (1, 4) slower
  • nose up: front (1, 3) faster, rear (2, 4) slower
  • yaw right: the CCW pair (1, 2) faster
  • outputs are limited to $[0, 1]$: a large combined command saturates a motor, and then the vehicle does not get what was asked

Part 3

Commanding a drone

Three command levels, each a feedback loop

the pilot commandsthe controller closes the loops onlearning timeexample modes
position (velocity) $x, y, z$ + yawposition, attitude, rateminutesArduPilot Loiter, PosHold; autonomous missions
throttle + angles (roll, pitch) + yaw rateattitude, rate~15 min with altitude holdStabilize, AltHold; "angle mode"
throttle + angular ratesrate onlyhoursAcro; FPV racing
  • the higher the level, the more of the stack the vehicle runs for the pilot, and the more it relies on estimates (position: GPS or vision; attitude: the IMU fusion of Part 4)
  • each level is a feedback loop, here a PID: a black box for today, the PID lecture covers it
  • it needs the current value; for the attitude loop that is the current roll, pitch, yaw

Angle mode or rate mode: what does the stick mean?

stick centred = pitch 0°; full stick = a maximum angle (e.g. 30°). Release the stick and the vehicle levels itself. A flip is impossible: the angle cannot pass the maximum.

stick centred = pitch rate 0: keep the current pitch, whatever it is. Hold the stick and the vehicle keeps rotating: flips are possible, but the pilot has to bring it back to level.

The cascade: position → attitude → rate → motors

  • each loop is faster than the one around it; like a car: position → velocity → acceleration → force
  • the split is a design choice (often position → velocity → attitude); loop rates are typical values, ArduCopter runs attitude and rate loops at 400 Hz, racing firmware the rate loop at up to 8 kHz

How a flip is flown, and why angle mode cannot do it

  • rate (acro) mode: climb, full pitch stick (the rate loop turns at a constant 360°/s; racing quads 700 to 1000°/s), cut the throttle upside down, centre the stick when level, catch with the throttle
  • the rate loop needs only the gyro, in the body frame: nothing special at the vertical
  • angle mode: the stick is an angle setpoint, limited to about 30°: the controller pulls back towards it
  • even without the limit, pitch beyond 90° does not exist in Euler angles: an angle controller would have to pass gimbal lock (next slides)

A flip passes the vertical: gimbal lock optional

  • Euler angles (ZYX): $R = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\varphi)$: heading, nose up, bank
  • point a phone's top straight up ($\theta = 90°$, "pitch → 90°"): turning it about the vertical (yaw) and about its own long axis (roll) is now the same motion
  • three angles, two independent ways to turn: gimbal lock; only $\psi - \varphi$ is defined
  • pitch runs only from −90° to 90°: past the vertical the same attitude gets completely different numbers
Flight controllers store the attitude as a quaternion (or a rotation matrix) and show Euler angles only to people.

Why gimbal lock is a problem optional

  • the drone turns smoothly; near the vertical its roll and yaw angles swing by about 140° in 0.2 s, and pitch turns back at 84° instead of going over the top
  • integrating the gyro in Euler angles: $\dot\psi = (q\sin\varphi + r\cos\varphi)/\cos\theta$ blows up; here a body rate of 180°/s needs $\dot\psi \approx 1600$°/s, at exactly 90° it is undefined
  • a controller on angle errors sees a jump of 180° as a huge error and commands a violent, wrong correction
  • at 90° many $(\psi, \varphi)$ give the same attitude: the estimate is not unique, noise flips it around
Every description of 3D rotations with 3 numbers has such a singularity somewhere. A quaternion (4 numbers, $\|q\| = 1$) changes smoothly: use it inside, Euler angles only for people and small tilts.

What the sensors actually measure

No sensor measures the attitude

gyroscopeangular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ (3 axes)fast, smooth, short termbias: integrating it drifts

magnetometerlocal magnetic field (3 axes)heading (yaw)disturbed by motors and steel (end of Part 4)
barometerair pressurealtitude changesdrifts with weather, prop wash

The IMU (inertial measurement unit) = gyroscope + accelerometer, a few-mm MEMS chip. Attitude is not in this table: it has to be computed from several of these signals.

Gyroscope: integrate the rate, collect the errors

$$\omega_{\text{meas}} = \omega + b + n, \qquad \hat\theta(t) = \int_0^t \omega_{\text{meas}}\,dt = \theta(t) + \underbrace{b\,t}_{\text{grows}} + \underbrace{\textstyle\int n\,dt}_{\text{random walk}}$$

A consumer MEMS gyro, after start-up calibration, still has a bias of order 0.01 to 0.1 °/s, and it changes with temperature. Excellent for a fraction of a second, useless as the only source for minutes.

Accelerometer: specific force, not acceleration

$$\mathbf f_{\text{body}} = R^\top(\mathbf a - \mathbf g) + \mathbf b_a + \mathbf n$$
  • it measures every force except gravity, per unit mass: the push of the table, the thrust, the drag
  • at rest: $\mathbf f = -\mathbf g$, a vector pointing up, so its direction in the body frame gives roll and pitch
  • rotating about the vertical does not change it: yaw is invisible to the accelerometer (that is what the compass is for)

In flight the accelerometer cannot see the tilt

  • tilt from the accelerometer = the angle between the measured vector $\mathbf f$ and the body's up axis. This assumes $\mathbf f$ points straight up, which is true at rest: the ground pushes up against gravity (left, 25° ✓)
  • in flight $\mathbf f = T/m$, and the thrust $T$ always points along the body's up axis: the angle is 0° at any tilt, the drone looks level (right). The real 25° shows up as a horizontal acceleration $g \tan 25° = 4.6$ m/s² instead
  • tilt and acceleration are indistinguishable from the accelerometer alone; the gyroscope (or a model of the motion) has to resolve it

Position from the accelerometer: errors grow with $t^2$

Integrate twice: a constant acceleration error $\delta a$ gives a position error $\tfrac12\,\delta a\,t^2$. A good calibrated MEMS accelerometer keeps a bias of the order of 0.01 m/s².

Worse: an attitude error $\varepsilon$ means subtracting gravity in the wrong direction, so $g \sin\varepsilon$ leaks into the horizontal axes: 1° ≈ 0.17 m/s². Position needs GPS, vision or another absolute reference.

Barometer and GPS: absolute, but slow and imperfect

  • barometer: air pressure, about 12 Pa less per metre up; altitude relative to the take-off point
  • noise about 10 cm; but 1 hPa of weather change ≈ 8 m, and the weather changes during a flight
  • prop wash, ground effect and airspeed change the local pressure: foam over the sensor, care near the ground
  • GPS: position and velocity, 5 to 10 Hz, about 100 ms late; 1 to 3 m horizontally in open sky, worse vertically; the error wanders slowly
  • velocity (from Doppler) is better: about 0.1 m/s
  • RTK with a base station: cm; multipath near buildings, nothing indoors, jamming

Magnetometer: the field is a 3D vector, mostly vertical

  • gravity fixes roll and pitch, not yaw; the Earth's field (about 50 µT) gives a second direction
  • here it points 68° into the ground; only its horizontal part shows north
  • so first rotate the reading to level, with roll and pitch from the IMU fusion; then heading = direction of the horizontal part, plus the declination $D$
1° of roll or pitch error leaks the vertical part into the horizontal: up to $\tan 68° \approx 2.5°$ of heading error. The compass depends on the attitude estimate.

What the compass sees while the vehicle turns

ideal sensor

hard iron: a magnet on the vehicle

the sensor measures in the body frame, $m = R^\top e$; rotated back with the attitude, $R\,m = e$: the yellow arrow never moves
a field $h$ fixed to the vehicle turns with it: the reading $e + R\,h$ wobbles around $e$, in length and direction; uncorrected, the heading can be off by tens of degrees

Motor currents and declination

  • a current makes its own field, proportional to the current: tens of amperes to the motors, changing with the throttle
  • supply and return wires side by side: the field falls with $1/r^2$, so a mast twice as far gives four times less
  • what is left is measured: ArduPilot's compassmot runs the motors, records the field change per ampere, and subtracts it in flight
  • the compass points to magnetic north; true north differs by the declination $D$
  • $D$ depends on the place (Warsaw 6.8°, Tallinn 9.6°) and slowly on the year; the autopilot looks it up in a table from the GPS position
  • an error in $D$ rotates every heading: flying by heading drifts sideways by $\sin \Delta D$ of the distance

Part 5

Why we need an estimator

State estimation is not a trivial problem

what we have

  • a model of the motion (rigid body, thrust along the body axis)
  • many sensors, each measuring something else, at different rates, with biases, noise and failure modes
  • no sensor for the quantities the controller needs

what we want

  • the best estimate of attitude, velocity, position (and the sensor biases)
  • with an uncertainty: how much to trust each sensor, right now
  • fast enough for the control loops
The simplest fusion blends the sensors with weights fixed by hand (a complementary filter). The Kalman filter derives the weights from the model and the noise levels, and keeps an uncertainty; the extended Kalman filter (EKF) in every flight controller does this for 20+ states. Next: control theory and the Kalman filter.

Homework 3 (drone through gates in a simulator): the state comes from a camera and a Kalman filter; task 4 lets you replace the simulator's true state with a simulated gyroscope and accelerometer.

Predict with the IMU, correct with the slow sensors

Between fixes the filter integrates the accelerometer and its uncertainty grows; each fix, even 100 ms late, pulls it back. The filter knows how much to trust each source, right now: the Kalman filter lecture formalises exactly this.

Fly it: the cascade on a noisy, delayed estimate optional

A planar quadcopter (side view, two motors) with the cascade of Part 3 and the limits of Part 2. With the true state, raise the gains: it gets faster. Switch to the estimate (delays, bias, noise like Part 4) and raise them again: it wanders, overshoots and rings around the target. How good the estimate is limits how good the control can be.

  • a plane is stable by its shape: the pilot only sets thrust and three control surfaces
  • a quadcopter has four motor speeds and no passive stability: it needs a much more sophisticated stack

Exercises

  1. A 1.5 kg quadcopter flies level-altitude with a 30° pitch. What total thrust does it need, and what is its horizontal acceleration?
  2. ArduPilot QUAD/X, hover at $T = 0.5$. Which motor saturates first if you command $P = 0.3$ and $Y = 0.3$ at the same time, and what roll, pitch and yaw do you actually get?
  3. A gyro has a constant bias of 0.05 °/s. What is the heading error after 10 minutes of integration?
  4. Why can a phone lying on a table tell which side is up, but not which way is north, from its accelerometer alone?
  5. A quadcopter with a 1 kg frame hovers 20 min on a 0.5 kg battery. You fit a 1 kg battery (same chemistry, nothing else changes). How long does it hover now, and what happened to its thrust-to-weight ratio if it was 2.7:1 before?

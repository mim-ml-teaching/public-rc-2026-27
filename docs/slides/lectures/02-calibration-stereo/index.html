The projection matrix $P$

Camera to pixel

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u\\ \tilde v\\ \tilde w\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x & 0\\ 0 & f_y & o_y & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_c\\ y_c\\ z_c\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

$\tilde{\mathbf u} = \textcolor{#f2b134}{M_{int}}\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_c, \qquad M_{int} = [K\,|\,0]$

World to camera

$$\begin{bmatrix}x_c\\ y_c\\ z_c\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & t_x\\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & t_y\\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & t_z\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ z_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

$\tilde{\mathbf x}_c = \textcolor{#f2b134}{M_{ext}}\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_w, \qquad \mathbf x_c = R(\mathbf x_w - \mathbf c_w) \;\Rightarrow\; t = -R\,\mathbf c_w$

Combining the two equations gives the full projection matrix $P$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf u} = M_{int}M_{ext}\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_w = K[R\,|\,t]\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_w = \textcolor{#f2b134}{P}\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_w$$
$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u\\ \tilde v\\ \tilde w\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & p_{24}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & p_{34}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ z_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \quad (u, v) = \Big(\frac{\tilde u}{\tilde w}, \frac{\tilde v}{\tilde w}\Big)$$

after S. Nayar, First Principles of Computer Vision

The projection matrix $P$: properties

  • $P = K[R\,|\,t]$ is $3\times4$
  • $P$ and $kP$ ($k \ne 0$) give the same pixels: defined up to scale
  • $\tilde w = $ depth $z_c$ of the point (when $P$ is normalised so that $\|(p_{31}, p_{32}, p_{33})\| = 1$)

The camera centre $\mathbf c_w$

The pinhole (the centre of projection, where all rays meet), given in world coordinates. In the camera frame it is the origin: $\mathbf x_c = R\,\mathbf c_w + t = R\,\mathbf c_w - R\,\mathbf c_w = \mathbf 0$, since $t = -R\,\mathbf c_w$. So

$$P\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf c_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = K\,(R\,\mathbf c_w + t) = K\,\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0$$

$(0, 0, 0)$ is not a homogeneous pixel: the centre is the only point in space without an image. Conversely, from a calibrated $P$ the centre is its null space: $\mathbf c_w = -R^\top t$.

Counting degrees of freedom

$P$: 12 entries, up to scale → 11 DoF.

$K$ with $f_x, f_y, o_x, o_y$: 4  +  $R$: 3  +  $t$: 3  =  10.

Which parameter is missing?

The skew $s$ in $K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: pixel axes not perpendicular.

Every $P$ whose left $3\times3$ block is invertible splits uniquely into $K[R\,|\,t]$ with this $K$ (positive diagonal): $4 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 11$.

Skew in practice

$s \ne 0$ means the pixel axes are not perpendicular. Not the case for digital sensors (OpenCV fixes $s = 0$); it appears when an image is warped with a shear, or for film scanned with a slightly slanted scan direction. We keep $s$ so that every $P$ splits into $K[R\,|\,t]$.

How to find the projection matrix?

Calibration procedure: steps 1–2

Step 1: capture an image of an object with known geometry.

Step 2: identify correspondences between 3D scene points and image points.

captured image (640 × 480 pixels)

Calibration procedure: step 3

For each corresponding point $i$, with $\mathbf{x}_w^{(i)} = (x_i, y_i, z_i)$ and pixel $(u_i, v_i)$:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}u_i\\ v_i\\ 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\textcolor{#5fd38d}{\text{known}}} \equiv \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & p_{24}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & p_{34}\end{bmatrix}}_{\textcolor{#f2b134}{\text{unknown}}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_i\\ y_i\\ z_i\\ 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\textcolor{#5fd38d}{\text{known}}} \qquad (\equiv\ \text{equal up to scale})$$

Expanding the matrix as equations:

$$u_i = \frac{p_{11}x_i + p_{12}y_i + p_{13}z_i + p_{14}}{p_{31}x_i + p_{32}y_i + p_{33}z_i + p_{34}}$$

$$v_i = \frac{p_{21}x_i + p_{22}y_i + p_{23}z_i + p_{24}}{p_{31}x_i + p_{32}y_i + p_{33}z_i + p_{34}}$$

Multiply out the denominators: linear in the unknowns $p_{jk}$

$$\begin{aligned} p_{11}x_i + p_{12}y_i + p_{13}z_i + p_{14} - u_i x_i\,p_{31} - u_i y_i\,p_{32} - u_i z_i\,p_{33} - u_i\,p_{34} &= 0\\ p_{21}x_i + p_{22}y_i + p_{23}z_i + p_{24} - v_i x_i\,p_{31} - v_i y_i\,p_{32} - v_i z_i\,p_{33} - v_i\,p_{34} &= 0\end{aligned}$$

Calibration procedure: step 4

Rearranging the terms: stack the two equations of every point.

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & z_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_1x_1 & -u_1y_1 & -u_1z_1 & -u_1\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & z_1 & 1 & -v_1x_1 & -v_1y_1 & -v_1z_1 & -v_1\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ x_i & y_i & z_i & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_ix_i & -u_iy_i & -u_iz_i & -u_i\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & x_i & y_i & z_i & 1 & -v_ix_i & -v_iy_i & -v_iz_i & -v_i\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ x_n & y_n & z_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_nx_n & -u_ny_n & -u_nz_n & -u_n\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & z_n & 1 & -v_nx_n & -v_ny_n & -v_nz_n & -v_n \end{bmatrix}}_{A\ (2n\times12):\ \textcolor{#5fd38d}{\text{known}}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} p_{11}\\ p_{12}\\ p_{13}\\ p_{14}\\ p_{21}\\ p_{22}\\ p_{23}\\ p_{24}\\ p_{31}\\ p_{32}\\ p_{33}\\ p_{34}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{p}:\ \textcolor{#f2b134}{\text{unknown}}} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0\\ 0\end{bmatrix}$$
$A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$.  12 entries, but $P$ is defined up to scale (next slide): 11 unknowns, 2 equations per point → at least 6 points, not all on one plane.

Scale of the projection matrix

The projection matrix acts on homogeneous coordinates. We know that

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u\\ \tilde v\\ \tilde w\end{bmatrix} \equiv k\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u\\ \tilde v\\ \tilde w\end{bmatrix} \qquad (k \neq 0 \text{ is any constant})$$

That is,

$$\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & p_{24}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & p_{34}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ z_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \equiv k\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & p_{24}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & p_{34}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_w\\ y_w\\ z_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

Projection matrices $P$ and $kP$ give the same pixels. So if $\mathbf p$ solves $A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$, so does $k\mathbf p$ — and $\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$ is always a (useless) solution. We must fix the scale.

Least squares solution for $P$

Option 1: set the scale so that $p_{34} = 1$

Option 2: set the scale so that $\|\mathbf p\|^2 = 1$

With noise, $A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$ has no exact solution. We want $A\mathbf p$ as close to $\mathbf 0$ as possible and $\|\mathbf p\|^2 = 1$:

$$\min_{\mathbf p}\ \|A\mathbf p\|^2 \quad \text{such that} \quad \|\mathbf p\|^2 = 1$$

$$\min_{\mathbf p}\ \mathbf p^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf p \quad \text{such that} \quad \mathbf p^\top \mathbf p = 1$$

A minimisation with an equality constraint: the method of Lagrange multipliers (next slides).

Why not $p_{34} = 1$?

It also fixes the scale, and then plain least squares works. But $p_{34} = (Kt)_3 = t_z$: if the world origin happens to be close to the camera's $z_c = 0$ plane, $p_{34} \approx 0$ and fixing it to $1$ blows up the other entries. The constraint $\|\mathbf p\| = 1$ has no such special case.

The Lagrangian method: intuition

Problem

$$\begin{aligned} \text{maximise}\quad & \textcolor{#f2b134}{f(x, y) = x^2 y} \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0} \end{aligned}$$

the constraint $g = 0$ is the unit circle

At the optimum the contour of $f$ touches the circle — if it crossed it, we could move along the circle to a higher contour. Touching = parallel normals:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\, \nabla g$$

Solutions: $(0, \pm1)$, $(\pm\sqrt{2/3}, \pm 1/\sqrt3)$.

Only candidates: evaluate $f$, pick the best.

From the intuition to the Lagrangian

Optimise $\textcolor{#f2b134}{f(x)}$ subject to $\textcolor{#5ab0ff}{g(x) = 0}$. At the optimum, two conditions:

  1. the contour of $f$ touches the constraint: $\;\nabla f(x) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x)$
  2. we are on the constraint: $\;g(x) = 0$

Pack both into one function of $x$ and $\lambda$, the Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal L(x, \lambda) = \textcolor{#f2b134}{f(x)} - \lambda\, \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{g(x)}$$

Its gradient is zero exactly when both conditions hold:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_x \mathcal L &= \nabla f(x) - \lambda\,\nabla g(x) = 0 &&\text{(1) touching}\\ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \lambda} &= -g(x) = 0 &&\text{(2) on the constraint}\end{aligned}$$

Solutions are candidates (max, min or neither): evaluate $f$, pick the best.

Example (previous slide)

$$\mathcal L = x^2 y - \lambda\,(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$$

$$\begin{aligned} \partial_x:&\ \ 2xy - 2\lambda x = 0\\ \partial_y:&\ \ x^2 - 2\lambda y = 0\\ \partial_\lambda:&\ \ x^2 + y^2 = 1\end{aligned}$$

→ the six candidates $(0, \pm1)$, $(\pm\sqrt{2/3}, \pm 1/\sqrt3)$

Our problem

$\textcolor{#f2b134}{f(\mathbf p) = \mathbf p^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf p}$ (minimise),  $\textcolor{#5ab0ff}{g(\mathbf p) = \mathbf p^\top \mathbf p - 1}$:

$$\mathcal L(\mathbf p, \lambda) = \mathbf p^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf p - \lambda\,(\mathbf p^\top \mathbf p - 1)$$

Constrained least squares solution

Taking derivatives of $\mathcal L(\mathbf p, \lambda)$ with respect to $\mathbf p$:   $2A^\top A\,\mathbf p - 2\lambda\,\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$

$$A^\top A\,\mathbf p = \lambda\,\mathbf p$$
eigenvalue problem

The eigenvector $\mathbf p$ with the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A^\top A$ minimises the loss.

Rearrange the solution $\mathbf p$ to form the projection matrix $P$.

Two gaps: why the smallest eigenvalue? And where does $\nabla_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf p^\top M \mathbf p) = 2M\mathbf p$ come from ($M = A^\top A$)?

Which eigenvector?

The Lagrangian gives only candidates: every unit eigenvector of $A^\top A$ satisfies $A^\top A\,\mathbf p = \lambda\,\mathbf p$ (12 of them).

Evaluate the loss at a candidate, using $\mathbf p^\top \mathbf p = 1$:

$$\|A\mathbf p\|^2 = \mathbf p^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf p = \mathbf p^\top (\lambda\,\mathbf p) = \lambda$$

The loss at each candidate is its eigenvalue → pick the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$.

In code: the last right singular vector of $A$, np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1] — the same vector, without forming $A^\top A$ (numerically better).

The derivative of a quadratic form

$$q(p) = p^\top M p = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} M_{ij}\, p_i\, p_j$$

Differentiate with respect to $p_k$: the terms with $i = k$ give $\sum_j M_{kj} p_j$, the terms with $j = k$ give $\sum_i M_{ik} p_i$:

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial p_k} = (Mp)_k + (M^\top p)_k$$

$$\nabla q = (M + M^\top)\,p = 2Mp \quad \text{for symmetric } M$$

Sanity check in 1D

$q(p) = m p^2$, $\;q'(p) = 2mp$.

Also useful

$\nabla_p (b^\top p) = b$,   $\nabla_p \|p\|^2 = 2p$.

So $\nabla_p \|Ap - b\|^2 = 2A^\top(Ap - b)$ — setting it to zero gives the normal equations $A^\top A p = A^\top b$ of ordinary least squares.

Extracting intrinsic and extrinsic parameters

We know that

$$P = \begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & p_{24}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & p_{34}\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x & 0\\ 0 & f_y & o_y & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}}_{M_{int}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & t_x\\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & t_y\\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & t_z\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{M_{ext}}$$

That is, the left $3\times3$ block:

$$\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13}\\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23}\\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33}\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\textcolor{#5ab0ff}{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x\\ 0 & f_y & o_y\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}}}_{\textcolor{#5ab0ff}{K}} \underbrace{\textcolor{#5fd38d}{\begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13}\\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23}\\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33}\end{bmatrix}}}_{\textcolor{#5fd38d}{R}} = KR$$

$K$ is an upper triangular matrix and $R$ is an orthonormal matrix: the product $KR$ can be uniquely split ("decoupled") into $K$ and $R$ by the RQ decomposition (QR "from the other side"; unique with a positive diagonal of $K$).

Extracting intrinsic and extrinsic parameters (2)

The last column:

$$\begin{bmatrix} p_{14}\\ p_{24}\\ p_{34}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x\\ 0 & f_y & o_y\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} t_x\\ t_y\\ t_z\end{bmatrix} = K\,t$$

Therefore

$$t = K^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} p_{14}\\ p_{24}\\ p_{34}\end{bmatrix}$$

Details

  • $P$ is known only up to scale (and sign): divide by $k_{33}$; if $\det$ of the left block is negative, flip the sign of $P$ first, so that $\det R = +1$
  • scipy.linalg.rq, cv2.decomposeProjectionMatrix
  • camera centre directly: $\mathbf c_w = -R^\top t$

Calibration to play with

DLT, and why one plane is not enough optional

DLT = Direct Linear Transform

What we just did (steps 3–4):

  • multiply out the denominators → equations linear in the entries of $P$
  • stack them: $A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$
  • solve directly: the eigenvector of $A^\top A$ with the smallest eigenvalue — no initial guess, no iterations

Used for many models: projection matrix, homography (lecture 3), triangulation.

All points on one plane: degenerate

Put the world frame on the board: every point has $z_i = 0$. The two rows of point $i$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_i & y_i & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{0} & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_ix_i & -u_iy_i & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{0} & -u_i\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & x_i & y_i & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{0} & 1 & -v_ix_i & -v_iy_i & \textcolor{#ff6b6b}{0} & -v_i\end{bmatrix}$$

  • the columns of $\textcolor{#ff6b6b}{p_{13}, p_{23}, p_{33}}$ (they multiply $z_w$) are zero in $A$: any values fit
  • $A\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$ has a whole subspace of solutions (several zero eigenvalues): $P$ is not determined, however many points we take
  • one view of a plane only gives the $3\times3$ map $(x, y, 1) \mapsto (\tilde u, \tilde v, \tilde w)$: a homography, 8 DoF < 11
DLT calibration needs a 3D target (the cube). A flat checkerboard needs several views in different orientations (Zhang's method, next slide). Try "one face only" in the calibration widget.

Calibration in practice optional

Zhang's method (what OpenCV does)

  • a flat checkerboard, easy to print — but one view of a plane does not determine $P$ (previous slide)
  • take 10–20 photos in different orientations; each view gives a homography (plane → image, lecture 3)
  • constraints from all views give $K$ in closed form
  • non-linear refinement: minimise the reprojection error over $K$, distortion and all board poses (Levenberg–Marquardt)

cv2.findChessboardCorners → cv2.cornerSubPix → cv2.calibrateCamera; ChArUco boards also work when partly occluded.

Algebraic vs geometric error

DLT minimises $\|Ap\|$, which has no physical meaning. What we care about is the distance in pixels between detected and reprojected points. Linear methods give the starting point; non-linear least squares finishes the job. Normalise the coordinates before DLT (Hartley): it is much more stable.

Good practice

  • tilt the board; cover the whole image, especially the corners (distortion)
  • fixed focus and zoom: refocusing changes $K$
  • check the RMS reprojection error (well below 1 px)
Part 3

Perspective-n-Point (PnP)

Where is the object?

Given: a calibrated camera ($K$ known), an object of known geometry (points $X_i$ in the object frame), their pixels $(u_i, v_i)$.

Find: the object pose $R, t$ in the camera frame — 6 DoF.

How many points do we need?
  • each point: 2 equations → 3 points (P3P)
  • 3 points: up to 4 solutions
  • a 4th point usually picks the right one

Where it is used

  • fiducial markers (ArUco, AprilTag): 4 corners → pose of the marker
  • object pose from a CAD model and detected keypoints
  • localisation in a known map; augmented reality
  • calibration: pose of the board in each view

Calibration: unknown $K$, 11 DoF, $\ge 6$ points. PnP: known $K$, 6 DoF, $\ge 3$ points.

Three points: up to four poses

Three rays, one known triangle: up to four ways to place it with its corners on the rays. A 4th point picks the right one.

PnP as least squares

With many points and noise, minimise the reprojection error:

$$\min_{R,\,t} \sum_i \big\|\, \pi\big(K(R X_i + t)\big) - (u_i, v_i) \,\big\|^2, \qquad \pi(a, b, c) = (a/c,\ b/c)$$

PnP to play with: how precise is the pose?

What to observe

  1. Depth is the weak direction: the cloud is long along $\Delta z$. At 0.5 m: σ ≈ 0.4 mm sideways, ≈ 2.6 mm in depth.
  2. A frontal marker has a poorly known tilt: distance 1 m, yaw 0° → tilt σ ≈ 9°, the dots form a ring around 0. Yaw 40° → ≈ 1.5°.
  3. Far away, the pose flips: distance 2 m (marker ≈ 40 px) → 40–50% of the solves jump to the mirrored tilt (red, on the red ring).
  4. Rotation about the normal is always precise (σ < 1.5°).

Remedies: larger or closer markers, view at an angle, more points, filtering over time.

Part 4

RANSAC

a way to deal with outliers

Outliers

measurements
→
inliers and the model fitted to them · outliers
  • correspondences come from detectors and matchers; some are simply wrong: repeated texture, reflections, occlusions (10–50% for feature matches, lecture 3)
  • least squares weights every point: one far outlier can move the solution arbitrarily far

RANdom SAmple Consensus

General RANSAC algorithm:

  1. Randomly choose $s$ samples. Typically $s$ is the minimum number of samples to fit a model.
  2. Fit the model to the randomly chosen samples.
  3. Count the number $M$ of data points (inliers) that fit the model within a measure of error $\varepsilon$.
  4. Repeat steps 1–3 $N$ times.
  5. Choose the model that has the largest number $M$ of inliers.

Finally: refit the model on all its inliers (least squares).

Minimal samples $s$

  • line: 2 points
  • PnP (P3P): 3 points
  • calibration (DLT): 6 points
  • homography: 4 points (lecture 3)

Fischler & Bolles, 1981.

How many iterations? optional

$w$: fraction of inliers, $s$: sample size, $p$: desired success probability (e.g. 0.99).

  • a sample is all inliers with probability $w^s$
  • all $N$ samples are contaminated: $(1 - w^s)^N$
  • we want $(1 - w^s)^N \le 1 - p$:
$$N \ge \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)}$$

Part 5

Simple stereo

Two cameras, two views

The same scene from two cameras side by side (the right one is 20 cm to the right).

Switch between the views:

  • close objects (the sphere, the box) jump a lot
  • far objects (the back wall) barely move

The shift (disparity) tells us the depth: the closer the object, the larger the shift.

Rectified stereo: the same row in both images

Definition

A stereo pair is rectified if its images look as if taken by two identical cameras (same $K$) with the same orientation, the right one shifted only along the $x$ axis of the left one, by the baseline $b$.

Disparity and depth

Point $(x, y, z)$ in the left camera frame; the right camera sits at $(b, 0, 0)$:

$$u_l = f\frac{x}{z} + o_x, \qquad u_r = f\frac{x - b}{z} + o_x$$

Same row in both images: $v_l = v_r = v$.

The disparity:

$$d = u_l - u_r = \frac{f\,b}{z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{f\,b}{d}$$

$$x = \frac{b\,(u_l - o_x)}{d}, \qquad y = \frac{b\,(v - o_y)}{d}$$

  • depth is inversely proportional to disparity
  • disparity is proportional to the baseline $b$
  • far away: $d \to 0$; at infinity $d = 0$ (the same pixel in both images)
  • near: large $d$ — very close points may be visible in one camera only

Rectified stereo

  • the plane through $P$ and both centres contains the baseline, so it cuts both image planes along the same row
  • the match of a pixel is searched along one row only
  • the two rays meet at $P$: triangulation — the formulas on the left

Rectification: warping the two images of a general pair (one homography each) into this form, cv2.stereoRectify. General case: lecture 3.

Depth precision degrades with $z^2$

Disparity is measured to a fraction of a pixel. How does an error $\Delta d$ affect depth?

$$\Big|\frac{\partial z}{\partial d}\Big| = \frac{f b}{d^2} = \frac{z^2}{f b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta z \approx \frac{z^2}{f\,b}\,\Delta d$$

Example: $f = 600$ px, $b = 10$ cm, $\Delta d = 0.25$ px:

$z$1 m5 m10 m
$\Delta z$4 mm10 cm42 cm

For a robot

  • stereo is precise for grasping at arm's length, coarse for distant obstacles
  • more precision at range: larger baseline or focal length (narrower field of view)
  • far away the two rays are almost parallel: the region of 3D positions consistent with both pixels (± the matching error) stretches along the ray (next slide)

Depth uncertainty to play with

Hunters and prey: where to put the eyes

Prey: eyes on the sides, almost 360° coverage, little stereo. Hunters: eyes forward, a wide binocular field for depth. Robots often do both: surround cameras for awareness, a forward stereo pair for precise depth.

Finding correspondences: block matching

  • rectified: the match of pixel $(u, v)$ lies on the same row $v$, at $u - d$ with $0 \le d \le d_{\max}$
  • compare a small window around $(u, v)$ in the left image with windows along the row in the right image
  • take the $d$ with the lowest cost ("winner takes all")

Window costs

over the pixels of the two windows $L$ (left) and $R$ (right):

  • SAD, sum of absolute differences: $\sum |L - R|$
  • SSD, sum of squared differences: $\sum (L - R)^2$

NCC, normalised cross-correlation: robust to brightness differences between the cameras.

A 2D search becomes a 1D search: this is why we rectify.

Block matching to play with

When matching fails

  • no texture (white wall): every window looks the same
  • repetitive texture (stripes, tiles): several equally good matches
  • occlusions: points seen by only one camera have no match
  • specular / transparent surfaces: appearance differs between the views
  • window size: small → noisy; large → blurred depth edges, and the window assumes a fronto-parallel surface (foreshortening)

Beyond block matching

  • semi-global matching (SGM): adds smoothness along several directions — cv2.StereoSGBM
  • active stereo: an infrared projector paints a random dot pattern, giving texture to white walls (e.g. Intel RealSense D400)
  • learned stereo: neural networks trained on large (often synthetic) datasets, e.g. RAFT-Stereo
  • other depth sensors: structured light, time-of-flight, lidar

Summary

  • $P = K[R\,|\,t]$, $3\times4$, 11 DoF; $t = -RC$
  • calibration (DLT): 2 linear equations per point, $\min\|Ap\|$ s.t. $\|p\| = 1$ → eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$; RQ → $K, R$
  • in practice: planar board, Zhang's method, refine the reprojection error
  • PnP: known $K$, 6 DoF, P3P gives ≤ 4 solutions; refine by least squares
  • RANSAC: minimal samples + consensus; $N = \log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s)$
  • rectified stereo: $z = fb/d$, $\Delta z \approx z^2\Delta d/(fb)$
  • correspondence along the row: block matching; fails without texture, with repetitions and occlusions

Appendix: rolling vs global shutter appendix

Global shutter: all pixels are exposed during the same interval, then read out. The image is one moment in time, as the pinhole model assumes.

Rolling shutter (most CMOS sensors: phones, webcams): rows are exposed and read one after another, top to bottom. Each row sees a different moment: rotating objects bend, moving objects skew.

For a robot: a moving camera skews the whole scene, which breaks calibration, PnP and visual odometry. Use a global-shutter camera, or model the time of each row.
